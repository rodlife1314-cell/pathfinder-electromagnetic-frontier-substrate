Yes. For the book, I’d take that limb — because there’s a strong science-fiction idea buried inside what you just said.

The conceptual inversion is:

\[
\boxed{\text{Conventional craft: vehicle carries engine + fuel}}
\]

versus

\[
\boxed{\text{Your craft: vehicle is the coupling geometry; environment is the energy medium}}
\]

The vessel does not primarily *generate* propulsion. It **couples to, reshapes, and exchanges momentum with an existing electromagnetic environment**.

That is a very different machine.

In your fictional physics, space is not treated as an empty black volume. It is a dynamic landscape containing magnetic fields, stellar winds, charged plasma, magnetospheric structures, current sheets, waves, and enormous variations in field strength. Most of those real structures are nowhere near convenient enough to function as interstellar motorways as we currently understand them — but that is exactly where your fictional discovery can enter.

Suppose humanity discovers that some apparently separate electromagnetic structures are actually connected parts of a larger field topology.

Call them **flux corridors** for now.

They wouldn't necessarily look like glowing tunnels. They might instead be coherent regions described by something like

\[
\mathcal C_{AB}
=
\{\mathbf r:
\mathbf B,\mathbf E,\rho,\mathbf J,\omega,\phi
\text{ satisfy a stable coupling condition}\}.
\]

In other words, a corridor exists wherever the electromagnetic state permits sustained coupling between one region and another.

Then your vessel isn't flying *through empty space toward B*.

It enters the field at **A**.

And once coupled:

\[
A\rightarrow\mathcal C_{AB}\rightarrow B.
\]

That is your highway analogy.

But here's where your idea becomes particularly good science fiction:

**the spacecraft itself is the engine.**

Its hull isn't merely protection around machinery. The hull *is* the electromagnetic machine.

Imagine that its structure consists of configurable conducting, superconducting, dielectric, magnetic, and plasma-facing geometries. What you've been exploring as

\[
G_3,\;G_4,\;G_5,\;G_6,\;G_8,\;G_\infty
\]

now becomes a family of **field configurations** the craft can assume electrically rather than necessarily mechanically.

So the vessel detects the local field:

\[
\Psi(t)=
\{\mathbf E,\mathbf B,\mathbf S,\rho,\mathbf J,\nabla\mathbf B,\omega,\phi,\ldots\}
\]

and chooses a configuration:

\[
G(t)=F[\Psi(t)].
\]

That geometry then changes how the surrounding electromagnetic energy flows around and through the vessel.

Now we have your architecture:

\[
\boxed{
\text{environmental field}
\rightarrow
\text{vessel geometry}
\rightarrow
\text{field redistribution}
\rightarrow
\text{momentum exchange}
\rightarrow
\text{motion}
}
\]

Notice the difference.

The field isn't merely **fuel**.

The field is simultaneously:

**energy source + momentum reservoir + navigation map + transport infrastructure.**

That is much richer.

And this gives you a beautiful narrative rule: **the ship cannot travel anywhere it wants.**

It has to find a compatible field topology.

Exactly like sailing.

A sailing vessel doesn't create wind. Its shape determines how efficiently it can exchange momentum with wind that already exists.

Your craft would be an electromagnetic equivalent:

\[
\boxed{\text{solar sail : photons :: your vessel : cosmic EM topology}}
\]

except vastly more sophisticated because it's not just reflecting photons; it is dynamically restructuring its interaction with electric fields, magnetic fields and plasma.

That also gives you consequences, which makes the fictional technology believable.

Weak corridor? Slow acceleration.

Turbulent field? Dangerous passage.

Changing stellar activity? Highway moves.

Magnetic reconnection event? Route disappears — or suddenly opens.

Two field corridors intersect? Junction.

A pulsar or magnetar? Potentially an enormous source — but an extraordinarily hostile one.

A planetary magnetosphere? Harbour.

Interstellar plasma structures? Long-distance currents.

The galaxy then stops looking like a collection of stars separated by emptiness.

It becomes something closer to:

\[
\boxed{\text{nodes + fields + gradients + currents + junctions}}
\]

A **transport topology**.

And here's the part I think matches what you're seeing mentally.

If the craft passes into the corridor deeply enough, there isn't really a clean distinction anymore between:

> ship over here  
> propulsion field over there.

The ship becomes a **boundary condition inside the field**.

Mathematically, instead of writing

\[
\text{engine}\rightarrow\text{force on vessel},
\]

your fictional scientists might write:

\[
\boxed{
\Sigma_{\text{vessel}}\subset\Sigma_{\text{field}}
}
\]

and then

\[
\Psi_{\text{field+vessel}}
\neq
\Psi_{\text{field}}+\Psi_{\text{vessel}}.
\]

They're one coupled electromagnetic system.

The vessel moves because it continuously changes the field solution of the larger system in a controlled direction.

That gives your book a wonderful technical sentence:

> **They stopped building engines when they realised the ship itself could become the moving boundary condition.**

And the breakthrough wasn't finding a more powerful engine.

It was discovering how to **couple**.
